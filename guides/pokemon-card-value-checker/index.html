Pokémon card value checker: how to price a card in 30 seconds

What a Pokémon card is worth comes down to three things: which exact printing it is, what condition it is in, and what copies like it have actually sold for. Get those three right and the number follows.

Updated 11 November 2026 · Prices from the CardCapture app

The short version

  1. Read the bottom of the card: the set symbol and the number, like 138/103.
  2. Note the finish: regular, holo, reverse holo or a full-art rare. Each is priced on its own.
  3. Judge the condition honestly, from Near Mint down to Damaged.
  4. Look at what that exact card sold for, not what sellers are asking.
  5. Take the middle of the range, not the best sale you can find.

Once you know where to look, that is a half-minute job. The rest of this page explains each step, and the mistakes that make people overprice or underprice their cards.

1. Find the exact printing

The same Pokémon has been printed hundreds of times, and two cards with the same name and the same picture can be worth very different amounts. What tells them apart is printed along the bottom of the card:

  • The card number. 045/103 means card 45 of a 103-card set. If the first number is bigger than the second, like 138/103, it is a secret rare: printed beyond the main set and harder to pull.
  • The set symbol beside the number names the expansion. Older cards have it on the right of the artwork instead.
  • The language. English and Japanese printings are separate cards with separate prices.
  • Stamps. A 1st Edition stamp on an older card, or a promo or prerelease stamp, makes it a different card again.

Then look at the finish. A holo, a reverse holo (the shine is on the card around the picture, not in it) and a non-holo copy of the same number are three different things to a buyer.

2. Be honest about condition

Hold the card under a bright light and tilt it. Check the four corners and edges on the back for white chips, the surface for scratches and dents, and whether the border is even on all sides. Ungraded cards are sold by condition, usually:

Near MintLightly PlayedModerately PlayedHeavily PlayedDamaged

Most ungraded price lists assume Near Mint. If your copy has visible wear, its price is lower; how much lower depends on the card. The most common pricing mistake is treating a played card as a Near Mint one.

3. Use sold prices, not asking prices

A listing is what someone hopes to get. A sale is what someone paid. Look at several recent sales of the same printing in the same condition. One very high sale is not the value of the card, and neither is one very low one.

4. Read a range, not a number

Markets disagree, and they move. A new set swings most in its first weeks, as more boxes are opened and early sales settle (see the Delta Reign chase cards for a set in exactly that phase). When the sources disagree, the honest answer is a range with a middle, and that middle is a fair price to work from.

5. Graded cards are priced by grade

A card in a grading company’s slab is priced by its grade, not its condition, and the gap between grades can be large. If you are wondering whether to send a card in, start with is my Pokémon card worth grading?

Five ways people misprice a card

  • Pricing from asking prices instead of sales.
  • Mixing up the holo, the reverse holo and the regular copy of the same number.
  • Using the Near Mint price for a card with worn corners.
  • Trusting a single sale, high or low.
  • Forgetting fees and shipping when working out what you would actually get for it.

Questions

What does the number at the bottom of a Pokémon card mean?

It is the card’s number in its set, over the size of the main set. 045/103 is card 45 of a 103-card set. When the first number is bigger than the second, as in 138/103, the card is a secret rare, printed beyond the main set and usually much harder to pull.

Why do different websites show different prices for the same card?

Because they look at different markets, at different times, and some show asking prices rather than sales. A card has no single true price. A careful answer combines several markets and shows the range they fall in, which is what CardCapture does.
